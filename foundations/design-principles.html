Foundations

Design Principles

Six principles. Each one is a decision tool, not a guideline — when two directions compete, the tests below choose. A principle that can't decide between two real options isn't doing its job.

01

Structure is the style

The Boekentoren is beautiful because its logic is visible. Our interfaces work the same way: spacing, type and hierarchy do the work, without ornament. Complexity inside our systems, teams and workflows stays inside them.

The test

Remove all colour from the screen. Does the hierarchy still read? If not, fix the structure before adding anything back.

Do

  • Use whitespace to create hierarchy
  • Let type scale carry the visual weight
  • Ask why something is decorated

Don't

  • Add colour where structure isn't working
  • Show users the seams between our systems
  • Use motion to make up for weak hierarchy
02

Opinions over options

We make the hard decisions so the user doesn't have to. We ask only what we need, once, and only from the person who owns the answer. A researcher's title capitalisation is the researcher's. A curator's schema is the curator's.

The first thing we decide is the surface. Public is about knowledge: what the research says, and whether you can read, cite and reuse it. Backoffice is about work: whether a record is correct, complete and compliant. Two audiences, two vocabularies. A screen that serves both serves neither.

Cutting works in both directions. Options nobody asked for are ours to remove. Responsibility is not. A deposit form stripped too far produces records too thin to curate, and the work lands on the curator instead — or on no one. Removing a question doesn't delete it, it moves it, so say where it went. In the other direction, we ask for what Biblio has to do: report to funders, make work findable, keep the record trustworthy. A field that can't name the goal it serves goes.

"Een keer informatie vragen is plagen, twee keer is pesten."

The test

For every field, modal or decision point, in this order. Which surface is this — knowledge or work? Then, whose job is it to decide? If it's ours, we've already decided. If it's the user's, we ask once and remember. If it's someone else's, it doesn't belong here.

Then, both directions: for each question you removed, who answers it now? For each you kept, which goal fails without it?

On the public surface, one more: would a reader with no repository vocabulary understand every word? "Open access" and "peer reviewed" pass. "A1", "AAM" and "deposit status" don't.

03

The past and the future share the same grid

Our collection spans centuries. Our researchers work at the edge of what's known. The system holds both without favouring either: it looks like neither a startup nor a museum. A 15th century manuscript and a 2024 preprint belong equally in every component.

We use standards because they help knowledge travel, not out of obligation.

The same goes for the harder half of the collection. Automatic import handles journal articles and preprints well. It handles book chapters, artistic output, software, policy reports and theses badly, and will for years. Those are the outputs the university most needs described, so their components get the same care as the easy ones.

The test

Put a 15th century manuscript and a 2024 open access preprint in the same component. Do both belong? Does it still work for an output type nothing will ever import automatically? And if we replace the visual layer in five years, does the structure hold?

04

Knowledge wants to move

We don't store knowledge, we move it. Every decision asks whether this makes the work easier to find, understand, share and build on.

Much of that movement isn't human. Citation managers, reporting tools and research evaluators read Biblio through the API, and so do the assistants readers now ask instead of a search engine. Structured data is not a secondary export, it is the main route. The interface is where a person can check that the data underneath is good.

We also design for the unexpected find: the thing someone wasn't looking for. And we share what we build and document, inside and outside the university.

The test

Count the steps from finding something to citing or sharing it. That number should keep dropping. From any item, can someone find something they didn't know to look for?

Then read the page as a machine: are the identifiers, relationships, licence and access level all there without parsing prose?

05

Trust is placed deliberately

Most metadata arrives without anyone typing it: pulled from identifiers and author profiles, generated, compared across sources, scored for reliability. We don't decide each value. We decide where trust sits — which sources are good enough to publish without review, which questions go to the researcher, which to the curator, and which the system answers alone.

A researcher and a curator are not answering the same question about the same suggestion. The researcher answers whether it is true of their work: is this mine, is this the right version, is this the affiliation I held then. The curator answers whether it works as part of a collection: correct, complete, compliant, comparable with everything beside it. Give each of them their own question. Ownership sets what someone is accountable for, not what they may look at — a researcher who wants to check the rest is welcome to, and some do.

Everything we publish was checked by someone. If not by us, then by the source it came from. That is what lets an imported value go public without anyone here reading it: a record from Zenodo carries its author's verification, and if the author got it wrong, that is theirs and the provenance says so. Demanding our own confirmation on top adds friction and buys accuracy we already had.

What we generate ourselves has no such source. An inferred affiliation, a suggested subject, a summary our systems wrote did not exist until we made it, and nobody has verified it — so publishing it unread would put our name on a claim no one has checked. Imported and unread can go public. Generated and unread waits for a person. Those two states must never look alike.

The test

For any value on screen: where did it come from, and whose question is it? Imported and unread is fine, as long as the record names the source and doesn't claim we checked it. Generated and unread never reaches the public surface. The failure is a screen where you can't tell which of the two you are reading.

Do

  • Show where a value came from where someone acts on it
  • Fill a suggestion in, and make accepting it one action
  • Give each role its own question, and let anyone look past it

Don't

  • Publish a value we generated before a person has read it
  • Make a researcher accountable for fields that are not theirs
  • Blank a field so someone retypes what we already know
06

Quality is reached in cycles

A record doesn't have to be right to be saved. We take incomplete metadata at entry and improve it through curation. A gate that blocks on completeness doesn't create quality, it moves the work and loses the depositor. The essential fields we ask for once and hold to. The rest can be rough, late, or filled in by someone better placed to know.

That only works if state is visible. Status, what changed, who changed it, and what is still open have to read at a glance — for the person who deposited and for the person who picks it up next.

The test

Does this form block on anything a curator could fix later? Then it should warn, not block. After saving something incomplete, can the depositor see what state the record is in and what happens next?

When principles conflict

Principles are useful because they pull against each other. The common conflicts:

01 vs 02 — Structure says remove it. Expertise needs it.

Principle 02 wins for expert users when the information really belongs to their workflow. But look first for a structural way to show it that doesn't add noise for everyone. Progressive disclosure usually answers it: the simple path first, depth for those who go looking.

01 vs 03 — Structure treats every record alike. Heritage items need more.

Resist special treatment. The system is a neutral container that treats every record with the same respect. If a manuscript needs more context than a preprint, that is a content problem: solve it with better metadata, not a different component.

01 vs 05 — Provenance is more chrome. Structure says remove it.

Provenance keeps permanent space where someone acts on it: a curator confirming a suggestion, a researcher checking who changed their affiliation, a reader judging whether a record has been checked. The rest — who typed which field when — is detail, available on request. The question is whether anyone acts on it.

02 vs 04 — We've decided. But should the user have this choice?

When the choice affects how knowledge spreads — open access, sharing, visibility — it is the user's. When it is about internal presentation or metadata formatting, it is ours, and we have already decided.

02 vs 05 — Confirming is asking. Didn't we decide not to ask?

A confirmation is not an option, it is a check on a decision already made. Treat it that way: show the value filled in rather than blank, make accepting quick, and group confirmations instead of spreading them through a form.

02 vs 06 — We ask once. The record came back incomplete.

Asking again is not asking twice. The first ask covers what only the depositor knows. A later one is a curator's question about a named gap, sent to the person who can close it, with the reason attached. What "een keer vragen" forbids is asking the same person the same thing because we lost their answer.

04 vs 06 — The record is incomplete. Does it go out?

The design half is settled: if it goes out, the page carries its state, and nothing on it implies a check that has not happened. Whether an incomplete record is publicly visible at all, and which gaps hold it back, is policy rather than design — an open question for Open Science Policy, not one to settle here.

05 vs 06 — Most records are provisional. Do we mark them all?

No. When provisional is the normal state, marking it everywhere marks nothing. Show state where someone can act on it or would be misled without it: a record's own page, a curator's queue, an export. In a list where every row would carry the same mark, the mark is noise.
